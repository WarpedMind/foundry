## 2026-08-18 — The remaining two follow-ups: conditional cross-skill offers, and a committed calibration fixture for `qc-review`

### An offer whose trigger the skill cannot observe does not ship, and the survivors are labeled judgment
- After two adversarial rounds — 16 findings on the offers, then 18 on those fixes — two offers were **deleted rather than repaired** (`qc-review`'s `/foundry-audit` option, `foundry-governance`'s `/qc-review` option), and the remaining eight were left as-is and recorded as judgment guidance in `CLAUDE.md`'s Rules. Rejected: a third patching round, and building a hook to make the offers mechanical.
- **Why deletion beat repair for those two:** each had been rewritten once already, and each rewrite produced a fresh instance of the same defect — a trigger that is true on every run of the skill, or one the skill cannot evaluate at all. `foundry-governance` reads no source at any of its five steps, so no code-facing condition it states is checkable by it; `qc-review`'s Step 5 is mandatory, so "findings were written" is not a condition. Two failed attempts at a discriminating trigger is the signal that none exists, not that the third will be better.
- **Why not a third round:** the pattern across ten and eleven is that each fix is new prose in the same register as the prose it replaces, with no artifact to inject a defect into — so no version can be shown capable of failing. That is precisely the property `skills/foundry-audit/SKILL.md` argues makes a check worthless as evidence, and reviewing prose as though rounds will converge treats it as a mechanism it is not.
- **Why not a hook:** these offers govern the contents of an `AskUserQuestion`, which is assistant behavior at a turn boundary, not a tool call a `PreToolUse` matcher can see. Hook 5 already holds the one genuinely mechanical position (push time) and is mutation-tested there. Building further hook surface would mean approximating a judgment with a keyword proxy — a check that passes while the defect is present, which this repo's standing Rule forbids outright.
- **How to apply:** before adding a conditional offer anywhere, ask whether the skill can determine the trigger's truth from its own numbered steps. `foundry-security` can (step 3 reports its scan result); `foundry-governance` cannot. If it cannot, the offer does not ship — do not weaken the trigger until it technically applies, which is how both deleted offers were originally justified. And never let this text be described as tested: it is instruction prose, `tests/run_fixtures.sh` cannot exercise it, and a green suite says nothing about it.
- **Enforced at:** the standing Rule added to `CLAUDE.md` (observability test plus the never-describe-as-tested clause); the recorded reasoning left inside `skills/qc-review/SKILL.md` and `skills/foundry-governance/SKILL.md`, each naming both failed attempts so a third is not made; `CLAUDE.md`'s KNOWN DEBT entry on non-convergence.

### A cross-skill offer is gated on what the turn touched, and three skills deliberately get none
- Every skill's `## Closing out` section now names `/qc-review` or `/foundry-audit` as an option, but each names a precondition with it. Rejected alternatives: adding both commands to every skill's option list unconditionally, and adding them centrally in one shared paragraph reused across the twelve files.
- **Why conditional:** an option that appears whatever happened carries no information. The user learns within a handful of turns that the menu always ends the same way, stops reading the last two entries, and the offer is then worth less than nothing — it has consumed screen space and trained a skim. The 2026-08-11 convention already anticipated this failure under the name anti-filler, and the same argument that forbids manufacturing a question when nothing is open forbids manufacturing an option when nothing is relevant.
- **Why per-skill wording rather than one shared block:** a reused paragraph would say the same thing in `foundry-stack` (where the trigger is a DECISIONS.md cross-reference standing in for an inline rationale) as in `foundry-security` (where it is a credential actually found in history). Those are different conditions with different reasons, and collapsing them produces text that names neither. The mechanical bar from 2026-08-11 — no sentence repeating across any closing section — was re-run over all twelve skills after this change and after every later edit to a closing section: 126 sentences, 126 distinct at the end of the session.
- **Why three skills get no offer at all, stated rather than left as an omission:** `foundry-help`, `oneshot`, and `promptify` each carry a paragraph saying so and why, because the standing Rule on conditional safeguards requires the negative branch to be written down — an unexplained absence is indistinguishable from an oversight, and the next reader would close it as a gap. The reasons are per-skill and had to be: a first draft justified all three with a single claim, that they read nothing and write nothing, which the adversarial round found false. `oneshot`'s Step 5 explicitly governs turns in which substantial work finished, and `/promptify!` continues into that work inside the same turn. The surviving reasons are narrower and true — Quick picks already recommends both commands, `oneshot` must stay portable to projects that have never heard of them, and `/promptify`'s Step 5a defers to the turn that runs the prompt. **The general lesson is the one to carry:** a single justification covering several cases is the shape to distrust, because it is written once and checked once while applying in places nobody re-read.
- **How to apply:** when adding a skill, ask what its turn actually touches before granting it either offer, and if the answer is nothing, write that down where the offer would have gone. Re-run the sentence-uniqueness extraction over every closing section after any edit to one; a pasted sentence is the first symptom of the boilerplate this is built to avoid.
- **Enforced at:** the `## Closing out` section of each of the eight skills that received a conditional offer; the explicit no-offer paragraphs in `skills/foundry-help/SKILL.md`, `skills/oneshot/SKILL.md` (Step 5), and `skills/promptify/SKILL.md` (Step 5a). No mechanical enforcement exists and none is claimed — this is instruction prose, and `tests/run_fixtures.sh` does not and cannot exercise it.

### The calibration deliberately departs from the skill it measures, at exactly one step
- The procedure runs `qc-review` through Step 4 and stops. Step 5 — append surviving findings to the project's CLAUDE.md KNOWN DEBT — is skipped, and the skip is written into the procedure rather than left to the operator.
- **Why:** run from the repo root as instructed, that CLAUDE.md is Foundry's own. The fixture's findings are fabricated by construction, and Step 5's label (`[QC review, <date>]`) is indistinguishable from a real one, so a faithful run would file five invented defects into a live debt list where a later reader has no way to tell them from genuine findings. The measurement would corrupt the thing being measured.
- **How this was found, which matters more than the fix:** the first two calibration runs did not do this — but by judgment, not instruction, and the log described them as having followed the skill as written. An operator reading the procedure literally would have gotten a different and damaging result, and nothing in the document would have warned them. A procedure whose recorded runs quietly deviate from it is not a procedure; it is a description of what one person happened to do.
- **How to apply:** when a test harness runs a real mechanism against synthetic input, trace what that mechanism *writes*, not only what it returns. The deviation needed here was invisible from the result — both runs looked perfect — and only appeared by reading the skill's own steps against the procedure's instruction to follow them.
- **Enforced at:** `tests/calibration/qc-review/README.md`'s step 1, which states the stop point and the reason; the note in `tests/calibration/qc-review/RESULTS.md` recording that rows 1 and 2 predate the written instruction.

### `qc-review`'s sensitivity gets a committed fixture, graded for partial credit, and never wired into CI
- `tests/calibration/qc-review/` holds a subject file with five planted defects, an answer key with per-defect grading rules, a procedure, and an append-only results log. Rejected: adding it to `tests/run_fixtures.sh`; scoring it as a single pass/fail; and rewriting `qc-review` itself.
- **Why a fixture at all:** a finding this skill reports is verifiable, because Step 4 requires reproducing it. A clean result is not. "Found nothing" and "did not look hard" are the same output, and re-running yields the same output again — the review is its own only witness. That is precisely the unfalsifiability `foundry-audit` escaped by being a script, and it cannot be escaped here: the judgment that lets this skill find an auth bypass is the same property that makes its silence unverifiable. Sessions 7-8 had the right instinct and planted defects, but did it once and committed nothing, so no later edit to the skill's own instructions has ever been measured against it.
- **Why it can never be a CI gate:** the review dispatches an LLM subagent — non-deterministic, billed, minutes long, and unable to run in GitHub Actions. Putting a non-reproducible result behind a pass/fail gate would make the suite flap for reasons unrelated to any change, and the predictable response to a flapping gate is to disable it. Worse, a green suite that silently omitted this would imply coverage that does not exist, which is the exact error the calibration exists to expose. `tests/run_fixtures.sh` therefore prints an explicit NOT COVERED line rather than staying quiet about the omission — silence there would be the absent-trigger-treated-as-safe shape this repo keeps finding.
- **Why partial credit rather than a threshold:** finding four of five is information about which class was missed, and a pass mark discards exactly that. The answer key grades per defect and records severity disagreements separately without counting them, because a severity that does not change what a reader does is not a sensitivity signal. The comparison that carries the weight is between logged runs, not within one: the same defect missed twice says something about the instructions, one missed once says little.
- **Why the fixture does not rewrite the skill:** the handoff was explicit and correct that the design was validated end to end in Sessions 7-8 and that nothing found since contradicts it. The gap was never the mechanism, only that its sensitivity was asserted rather than periodically demonstrated.
- **How to apply:** run it after editing `skills/qc-review/SKILL.md` (especially Steps 2 and 3), when the subagent's model changes, and before leaning on a clean review for a release or handoff. Log every run including the bad ones — a calibration whose poor results go unrecorded manufactures a reliability record out of selective reporting. Keep the answer key out of the reviewing subagent's context; that control is procedural, not sandboxed, and is stated as a known bias rather than presented as rigor.
- **Enforced at:** `tests/calibration/qc-review/README.md` (procedure, known biases), `tests/calibration/qc-review/EXPECTED.md` (answer key and grading rules), `tests/calibration/qc-review/RESULTS.md` (the log); the "Periodic calibration" section of `skills/qc-review/SKILL.md`; the NOT COVERED notice at the end of `tests/run_fixtures.sh`; the standing Rule added to `CLAUDE.md`.

## 2026-08-18 — Two follow-ups from the foundry-audit handoff built; two left for another session

### `/foundry-help` is on-demand only, deliberately not a third automatic session-start message
- The obvious-looking alternative — wire a "here's what Foundry is" explainer into the SessionStart hook alongside Hook 3 — was rejected before being built, not discovered as a problem afterward.
- **Why:** two things this repo already settled make that alternative worse than doing nothing. First, Hook 3's three-state design (scaffolded/dismissed/neither) exists specifically to avoid nagging (see this file's 2026-06-28 entry) — a second automatic message at the same moment either duplicates Hook 3's offer or competes with it for attention, reintroducing the exact noise that decision rejected. Second, Session 20 found the VSCode extension's chat panel has no documented UI element for rendering a SessionStart hook's `additionalContext` as visible text at all — an automatic explainer wired the same way would be invisible in precisely the interface where a new user most needs "what is this" answered. An on-demand command sidesteps both: no nagging to guard against since it only runs when asked, and its output is a normal assistant response rather than hook `additionalContext`, so it renders on every platform including VSCode.
- **How to apply:** if a future proposal suggests surfacing more Foundry information automatically at session start, check it against both of these before building — Hook 3's noise argument and Session 20's VSCode-invisibility finding are not specific to this one skill, they're general reasons the SessionStart slot is expensive real estate that already has an owner.
- **Enforced at:** `skills/foundry-help/SKILL.md`'s "Why this is on-demand only" section, which states both reasons rather than asserting the design choice unsupported; `CLAUDE.md`'s Architecture entry for `skills/foundry-help/`.

### Hook 5 (push-time qc-review offer) fires on `git push`, not `git commit`, and defaults to firing when risk can't be determined
- The handoff scoped this precisely — push not commit, offer not auto-run, once per session — and this entry records the two judgment calls the scoping still left open: what counts as "risky enough to offer," and what to do when that can't be determined at all.
- **Why this is not the `PostToolUse` auto-run rejected 2026-06-28:** that rejection was about firing on every edit to a risky file — wrong granularity (re-reviews the same half-finished function repeatedly) and structurally unable to block (the edit already happened). A `git push` is a real completion boundary, not a mid-edit one, and this hook uses `PreToolUse` specifically because it *can* run before the push's effects are irreversible — though it still only offers, chosen deliberately over blocking (see below).
- **Why offer, not block, even though `PreToolUse` can block here:** blocking a push pending a review would make this hook's own risk classification — a heuristic keyword match, not `qc-review`'s actual judgment — the thing standing between a user and their own push. A heuristic wrong in the blocking direction is a real cost (a legitimate push refused or delayed on a false positive); a heuristic wrong in the offering direction costs one ignorable message. The asymmetry only favors blocking if the classifier is trustworthy enough to gate on, and a filename-keyword grep isn't.
- **Why undetermined risk (`git diff` against `@{u}` fails — no upstream yet, detached HEAD) defaults to firing rather than staying silent:** this is the same failure shape this repo has now named explicitly as a standing Rule — absence of a trigger condition treated as unknown, not as the safe case. Here the asymmetry is unusually clean because the once-per-session cap makes the cost of guessing wrong in the "fire" direction bounded (at most one unwanted message for the entire session), while guessing wrong in the "stay silent" direction means a real risky push gets no offer at all, for a hook whose only job is to offer.
- **Why the once-per-session cap is spent only by an offer that actually fires, not by every push:** a non-risky push must not consume the session's one shot, or a later risky push in the same session would go unoffered — verified directly (a non-risky push followed by a risky one in the same session fires on the second, not silently on neither).
- **How to apply:** if this hook's risk-keyword list or cap logic changes, re-verify against `tests/run_fixtures.sh`'s Hook 5 section the same way the secrets-guard regex is re-verified — this suite was proven capable of failing (not just passing) by neutering the detection regex, the cap check, and the risk classifier one at a time before trusting a green run.
- **Enforced at:** `skills/foundry-hooks/SKILL.md`'s Hook 5 section (mechanism plus explicit negative-branch analysis); `tests/run_fixtures.sh`'s six inline Hook 5 cases; `skills/qc-review/SKILL.md`'s cross-reference, so the PostToolUse-vs-PreToolUse distinction is visible from either file.

## 2026-08-17 — foundry-audit built: a check that can't be shown to fail isn't a check

### The audit's logic lives in a script, breaking the "SKILL.md prose only" pattern every other skill follows
- `skills/foundry-audit/audit.sh` is the first real program in this repo's skill layer — every other skill is instructions for an assistant to execute. Considered and rejected: keeping the checks as prose (most consistent with the existing pattern, and with CLAUDE.md's own "no compiled code" stack line), and generating a per-project copy of the script into each scaffolded project.
- **Why:** the deciding argument isn't consistency or convenience, it's falsifiability. This skill's entire value claim is that mechanical checks catch what reading misses — and a prose check has no artifact to inject a defect into, so it can never be demonstrated capable of failing. That makes it precisely the unfalsifiable verification the 2026-08-17 template rule (added hours earlier, same day) forbids. The per-project copy was rejected on the staleness argument `foundry-update` exists for: every copy freezes at the day it was written. A secondary reason matters too — an assistant asked to "check every referenced path exists" writes a slightly different command each run, so the output varies with who ran it, and the clean runs still get counted.
- **How to apply:** the split is the rule, not the script. Anything mechanically checkable goes in `skills/foundry-audit/audit.sh` *with* a mutation case. Anything needing semantic judgment (is this absolute rule the same as that one, does this pasteable prompt stand alone) stays out of the script entirely and is labeled judgment in `SKILL.md`, so it can't inherit the tested checks' credibility. Don't add a check to the script that can only be approximated by a keyword-presence proxy — a proxy that passes with the defect present is the failure being guarded against, wearing the costume of a fix.
- **Enforced at:** `tests/run_fixtures.sh`'s `foundry-audit` suite (30 mutation cases); the standing Rule added to this repo's CLAUDE.md forbidding a check without one; `CONTRIBUTING.md`'s pre-PR checklist.

### The mutation harness was itself proven falsifiable, because it went green on the first run
- All 23 cases in the suite at that point (it has since grown) passed the first time they were run end to end. Rather than treat that as success, two checks in `skills/foundry-audit/audit.sh` were deliberately neutered — the dangling-decision-reference comparison and the code-fence parity test — and the suite re-run each time to confirm the matching case went red, then restored.
- **Why:** a test suite that passes immediately is indistinguishable from one that isn't running, and this repo has already been burned by exactly that shape (Session 4's "tested" claims in skill prose that turned out to cover one happy path). The whole skill argues that a green result means nothing until you've seen the mechanism produce a red one; applying that to the checks but exempting the harness that runs them would leave the argument one level short of where it needed to go.
- **How to apply:** when a new test suite passes on its first complete run, break the thing it tests before believing it. This is cheap — two edits and two re-runs here — and it's the only evidence that distinguishes "nothing is wrong" from "nothing is being examined."
- **Enforced at:** documented in `skills/foundry-audit/SKILL.md`'s "What was verified" section, which states the harness-level check alongside the per-check ones rather than only listing the checks.

### Precision was chosen over recall on the path check, after the first real run showed why
- Pointed at Foundry's own documents, the audit reported 35 broken file paths. Roughly 20 were noise: these docs discuss filenames as *subject matter* (`.gitignore` pattern examples, secrets-guard fixture names like `service-account.json`), not as references. The check now escalates only paths whose first segment names a real top-level entry in the repo, and groups everything else into one scannable INFO line.
- **Why:** the tempting read is that a 40% hit rate is acceptable for a thorough tool. It isn't, and the reason is behavioral rather than statistical — a findings list that's mostly noise gets skimmed once and ignored permanently, at which point the one real finding at position 31 is as undetected as if the check had never run, minus the effort spent. This mirrors the reasoning already recorded for `qc-review`'s rejected `PostToolUse` auto-run: a mechanism that fires constantly at the wrong granularity is not a more thorough version of one that fires well.
- **How to apply:** the trade has a real cost and it's stated in the skill's limitations rather than buried — a reference into a directory that was itself renamed away now lands in the quiet group instead of failing. Accepted deliberately. If a false positive appears in future use, fix `skills/foundry-audit/audit.sh` and add a mutation case, or add the path to `.foundry-audit-allow`; do not edit the document to satisfy the tool, which would make it measure itself.
- **Enforced at:** `skills/foundry-audit/SKILL.md`'s Known Limitations; the two precision-guard mutation cases in `tests/run_fixtures.sh` ("bare example filename is not a broken reference", "example path under a directory this repo lacks is not a finding").

### A check whose input set is empty reports SKIP and counts as a finding, never PASS
- Five result states rather than pass/fail: PASS, N/A (the convention genuinely doesn't apply — no ADR IDs in this project), SKIP (the check should have applied but its input was unexpectedly empty), FAIL, INFO. SKIP counts toward the exit code; N/A does not.
- **Why:** this repo has now found the same failure shape nine times — absence of a trigger condition silently treated as the safe case (Hook 3's empty-vs-false, `foundry-stack`'s missing governance section, `foundry-init`'s missing `.gitignore`, the README discipline's own skip path). A verification tool that reproduced it would be the worst possible place for it: "no decision headings found, therefore no dangling references" is a clean report from a check that examined nothing. Distinguishing N/A from SKIP is what keeps this from becoming noise in the other direction — a project that simply doesn't use ADR numbering shouldn't be told it has a problem.
- **How to apply:** any new check needs both branches decided before it's done, per the standing Rule: what does it report when its input exists, and what does it report when its input is unexpectedly absent. Three such guards are mutation-tested (unparseable decision headings, ADR refs with no definitions, no entry-point file for reachability).
- **Enforced at:** `skills/foundry-audit/audit.sh`'s state reporting; the three empty-input mutation cases in `tests/run_fixtures.sh`.

### Findings get fixed in the pass; only deferrals reach KNOWN DEBT — the opposite of qc-review
- `qc-review` reproduces a CRITICAL/HIGH finding before writing it into KNOWN DEBT. `foundry-audit` does not, and instead fixes mechanical findings in the same pass (after showing them and getting a yes), reserving KNOWN DEBT for what's genuinely deferred, labeled `[foundry-audit, <date>]`.
- **Why:** the verification bar differs because the thing being verified differs. A subagent's claim that code is broken is only a claim, which is why `qc-review` reproduces it. A mechanical finding has already reproduced itself — it names a file, a line, and a string that is or isn't present, and re-deriving that adds nothing. What needs verifying here is the *check*, and that happens once in the fixture suite rather than once per run. The second reason is practical: most findings are one-line fixes, and routing a stale path through KNOWN DEBT spends more attention than the defect does.
- **How to apply:** the one thing carried over from `qc-review` unchanged is that nothing gets written without being shown first — this skill reads documents and must not edit them silently, the same guard `foundry-docs` applies to overwrites.
- **Enforced at:** `skills/foundry-audit/SKILL.md` Step 4.

### The adversarial pass on foundry-audit found a bug that manufactured findings, not one that missed them
- An immediate ninth review round, pointed at the just-built `skills/foundry-audit/audit.sh`, probed the negative branches written in the previous hour. Five probes, three real findings: a document path containing a space word-split the doc set (one file counted twice, then reported unreachable four times over); a missing value for `--root`/`--doc` died on bash's own unbound-variable error and exited 1; and the fix for the first introduced a loop that read stdin and hung the whole fixture suite. Two suspicions were probed and *withdrawn* — a bad `--root` already exited 2 correctly (my first reading was a pipe artifact from `| tail`), and a table inside a code fence was already correctly ignored.
- **Why this is worth its own entry:** every prior finding in this repo's nine review rounds was a check that stayed silent when it should have fired. This one is the opposite and it is worse — a verification tool that invents findings destroys trust faster than one that misses them, because the user learns the output is unreliable and stops reading it, which disables the checks that do work. It is also the same shell word-splitting class already fixed once here (Session 6, the SessionStart hook templates), reappearing in new code by the same mechanism: a space-separated string used as a list.
- **How to apply:** keep list-shaped data in newline-delimited files and iterate with `while IFS= read -r`, never a space-joined string, anywhere a value could come from a filesystem path. And when converting a `for` loop to a `while read` loop, check the `done` — the hang came from a converted header whose terminator kept no redirect, so it silently consumed the script's stdin. Withdrawing the two false suspicions matters as much as fixing the three real ones: measuring an exit code through a pipe measures the pipe.
- **Enforced at:** two regression cases in `tests/run_fixtures.sh` (a spaced document path counted once; usage errors exiting 2). The hang needs no dedicated case — it blocks the existing suite outright, which is how it was caught.

### Two proposal claims were checked and found wrong, and one proposal check was deliberately not built
- The proposal's check table assumes `ADR-NNN` decision identifiers and an index; Foundry has neither (zero occurrences in `DECISIONS.md` or `templates/DECISIONS.md.template`). It also claims the reference implementation's only project-specific parts are the decision-ID regex and a file allowlist; in fact 5 of its 12 checks hardcode one project's counts, filenames and rule strings. Separately, two rows of that table — "every standards bullet is echoed somewhere actionable" and "pasteable prompts are self-contained" — were not implemented at all.
- **Why:** recorded because both wrong claims were load-bearing, and designing around either would have produced a skill that checks a convention this project doesn't use while missing the one it does. Date-headed entries are Foundry's actual decision convention, so date-reference resolution plus the newest-first ordering the file's own header asserts are the real checks; ADR support ships as an auto-detected extra rather than the primary. The two unbuilt rows both require judging whether one passage semantically covers another; the only mechanical approximation is keyword presence, which would pass while the defect is present — a check of exactly the kind this skill exists to replace, so shipping it would have been self-defeating rather than merely incomplete.
- **How to apply:** the same standing rule that produced this — verify a proposal's claims about current state against the actual files before designing around them — applies to every future proposal document, including ones written by an instance with more context than the reader. Not building something and saying so is a result; the unbuilt rows are listed in `SKILL.md`'s verification table as **not implemented**, with the reason, rather than quietly omitted.
- **Enforced at:** `skills/foundry-audit/SKILL.md`'s "What was verified, and how" table, which marks each proposal row as mutation-tested, judgment, or not implemented.

### Decision entries gained an optional `Enforced at:` field instead of a mandatory format change or an orphan-check mechanism
- The proposal's motivating case: an audit of a real project found a locked, correct decision ("no product name may be hardcoded") that the very next unit of work silently violated, because nothing connected the decision to where it should have been checked. ## 2026-08-11 — Ending a turn with a structured question is now a Foundry-wide convention

### Every skill closes its turn with an `AskUserQuestion`, not with open-ended prose
- The user reported from sustained live use that a turn ending in 2-4 concrete next-step options (plus `AskUserQuestion`'s built-in "Other") is materially better than one ending in prose, because picking is cheaper than composing. A `## Closing out` section was added to all nine skills.
- **Why:** the round-trip cost is asymmetric. Prose like "let me know what you'd like next" makes the user reconstruct the available next steps from a transcript they just read, while the skill already knows exactly which of its own steps remain unrun. This is the same mechanism DECISIONS.md's 2026-08-10 entry describes from the other direction — a list of options defines what the outcome can be, and anything left off it is effectively not on the table.
- **How to apply:** the closing question offers the steps this specific skill leaves open (e.g. `foundry-security`: run the committed-secrets check / wire the hook / generate `.env.example` / stop). Don't add a catch-all option — "Other" is supplied by the tool. Don't offer a destructive step as one casual choice among several; the existing confirmation sequencing for those still governs.

### The audit found the convention didn't exist anywhere yet, including in the two skills assumed to have it
- Premise going in was that `foundry-init` and `foundry-docs` already did this and the other seven didn't. Both do call `AskUserQuestion`, but mid-flow only — `foundry-init` for the questionnaire, `foundry-docs` for the per-file overwrite decision. Neither closed a turn with one, and `promptify` asked its "run it / edit it / another pass" question (Step 5a) in prose despite using the tool four times earlier in the same file.
- **Why:** worth recording because the distinction is invisible from a grep count, which is how the gap survived. A skill that uses the tool heavily mid-flow looks identical to one that closes with it, so "which skills do this already" cannot be answered by counting mentions — only by reading how each file ends.
- **How to apply:** all nine now carry the section, including the three that already used the tool. Mid-flow asks were left exactly as they were; the convention governs the close of the turn, not the interior.

### The convention also goes into the CLAUDE.md template, scoped to future scaffolds only
- Added as a standing Rule in `templates/CLAUDE.md.template`, which means it governs assistant behavior in every *newly* scaffolded project rather than only during a `/foundry-*` skill run. The user's scoping was explicit: "this only needs to affect projects from now on."
- **Why:** that scope is what the template mechanism already does by construction — templates render once at `/foundry-init` time, so an already-scaffolded project's CLAUDE.md is untouched by a later template edit. Retrofitting existing projects would need `foundry-update` (still on the Roadmap), and doing it any other way would mean editing files in projects Foundry no longer owns.
- **How to apply:** the template rule absorbs playbook entry 9c-iv rather than leaving the two as halves that assume each other — a recommendation must appear as a labeled option, not as prose above the question. It also carries the guard the skill-level sections carry, in the general form: general project work has no fixed list of remaining steps the way a skill does, so the risk of a filler menu ("continue / review / stop") is higher there, and the rule states plainly that an obvious next step is just taken, and a one-sentence answer stays one sentence.

### Per-skill wording, not shared boilerplate, and an explicit guard against question inflation
- Each section names that skill's own options and states the tension in its own terms. Checked mechanically: no sentence repeats across the nine sections.
- **Why:** identical text pasted nine times degrades into something skimmed and ignored, and the useful content here is precisely the part that differs — the options. The tension is real and had to be written down rather than left implied: a standing convention to end with a question sits directly against the standing guidance to pick the sensible default and proceed, and without a guard it would license asking about things with obvious answers, which costs the round-trip this convention exists to save.
- **How to apply:** each section states the limit in the form that bites hardest for that skill — `qc-review` must not manufacture a question when a review found nothing (its Step 3 already forbids padding), `foundry-governance` must not let a "fill this in" option become a back door around the anti-fabrication rule, `foundry-repo-hygiene` must not phrase an option as pre-authorization to commit, `foundry-stack` must not offer "mark it in use" without the evidence its verification section requires. Adding a tenth skill means writing a new section, not copying one.

## 2026-08-10 — Structured decision points: two failure modes found in live use

### A recommendation left out of the options list is not really on the table
- Observed across one long external-project session: the assistant recommended the same course correction three times, in prose, while every option offered in the accompanying question prompt was a variation on continuing the current work. It was selected zero times and never argued against. Added as playbook entry 9c-iv.
- **Why:** a list of options functions as a menu and defines what the outcome can be; prose around it reads as commentary. Selecting is far cheaper than composing a reply that overrides the visible choices, so an unlisted recommendation is effectively absent. This bites hardest when the recommendation is to *stop or change track*, which never has a natural home in a list of next steps.
- **How to apply:** a genuine recommendation must be the first option and labeled as such, not prose above the question. If the same recommendation goes untaken twice, drop the alternatives and ask about it alone. Distinguish "considered and declined" from "never actually presented" — the two look identical in a transcript and only the first is a decision.

### The "(Recommended)" label is a promise, and spending it carelessly destroys the mechanism
- Raised directly by the user: they had begun re-asking "is that actually your recommendation?" because they suspected the label was attached by habit or deference rather than judgment. Added to playbook entry 9c-iv.
- **Why:** once the label is not trusted, every question costs an extra round-trip to re-confirm the exact thing the label existed to communicate — making it worse than no label at all. The specific corrupting pattern is letting the recommended option drift toward whatever the person appeared to want, which converts a judgment into an echo and is nearly undetectable from a transcript.
- **How to apply:** label an option recommended only when you would defend it if challenged and would give the same answer with no options in front of you. A question with no genuine best answer gets no recommended option — presenting taste-based choices flat is correct, not unhelpful. If the reason cannot be stated in one clause, either move it to prose or drop the label.

### Self-directed process criticism needs the same evidence standard as any other claim
- Same session: the assistant judged mid-session that it was asking too many questions per prompt (three to four) and proposed narrowing to one or two. Checking the actual answers showed the person had engaged substantively with every sub-question, including the fourth. The advice was wrong and was recorded as wrong rather than acted on.
- **Why:** an assistant's discomfort with the *shape* of its own output is not evidence about that output's usefulness, and it reads as thoughtful self-correction, which makes it unusually likely to be adopted unchecked. Foundry's verify-before-trust rule applies to claims about one's own process, not only to external ones.
- **How to apply:** before changing a working practice on self-criticism alone, look for the evidence — here, whether the extra questions were actually answered. Applies to any "I should do less of X" instinct.

Don't let "this would be useful" by itself justify expanding Foundry's scope — check whether the capability is actually a scaffolding concern first.

### Do not trust "tested" claims in skill prose without re-verifying the actual command
- The secrets-guard hook's SKILL.md claimed it was "tested against a scratch repo: blocks when `.env` is staged, allows when only safe files are staged" — true for that one case, but an independent review found the regex missed `secrets.env`, `.env.production.local`, `config.yaml.bak`, and `real.key.txt`, all realistic filenames.
- **Why:** a narrow test that only checks the one obvious case can pass while the underlying logic is still broken for adjacent realistic inputs. The prose claim of "verified" created false confidence in exactly the same way `foundry-governance`'s anti-fabrication rule warns against for compliance sections — an unverified-but-confident claim is worse than an honest "not yet tested," because nobody thinks to double-check it.
- **How to apply:** going forward, any claim of "tested"/"verified" in a SKILL.md must be backed by a real adversarial fixture set (both false-negative and false-positive directions), not a single happy-path check — and that fixture set should be described in the SKILL.md itself (as the secrets-guard and `.gitignore` sections now do) so a future reader can see exactly what was checked, not just trust the word "verified."

### Commission a genuinely independent reviewer before public release, not just a self-review
- After fixing two destructive-action gaps the user personally caught (existing-file overwrite, wrong-directory scaffolding), did a careful self-review pass, then spawned a fresh subagent with zero prior context to independently hunt for the same class of risk.
- **Why:** the agent that built a system is structurally bad at finding its own blind spots — by the time you've written something, you've already convinced yourself the obvious failure modes are handled, which is exactly when you stop looking for non-obvious ones. A reviewer with no context, no investment in the existing design, and explicit instructions to be skeptical caught real things (a second instance of the regex/glob gap, a lossy-migration risk in full re-renders, sequencing ambiguity between two skills, a missing recovery path for outgrown "minimal" projects) that two rounds of this same builder's own review had missed.
- **How to apply:** before any future public release or major version of Foundry (or any other project), commission this same pattern — a fresh, context-free, explicitly skeptical review — rather than relying solely on the builder's own re-read, no matter how careful that re-read feels in the moment.

### Every finding gets fixed or explicitly, visibly deferred — never silently dropped
- The independent review produced 14 findings of varying severity. Given the volume, there was a real temptation to fix the obvious top few and let the rest fade into "we looked into it."
- **Why:** the user explicitly asked for this not to happen ("cover our butts in documentation as well") — and the cost of silently dropping a known-but-unfixed risk is that nobody, including future sessions, will know it was ever found, so it never gets revisited.
- **How to apply:** every finding from a safety/security review gets one of two outcomes, both visible: fixed now (with verification), or explicitly added to README.md's Roadmap with the reason it's deferred and which review caught it. Nothing gets quietly forgotten.

## 2026-06-28 — Post-launch additions (same day as initial build)

### STACK.md built in full now, not as a "lite" placeholder for a future tool
- The user already has a working manual pattern (a STACK.md from one of their other projects) and asked whether Foundry should incorporate a simplified version now, pending a separate future job-hunting tool, or build it properly now.
- **Why:** the per-project stack record is the data layer a future aggregation/job-hunting tool would need anyway — building it as a deliberately simplified placeholder now would mean either re-deriving it properly later (wasted work) or the future tool working from sloppy data (worse outcome). The cross-project master rollup (aggregating multiple repos) is the part that's genuinely a different, separate tool's job — that stayed out of scope, but the per-project piece did not.
- **How to apply:** when a "simplified version now vs. separate tool later" question comes up, check whether the "simplified" version is actually a different thing (skip it / build the full thing) or really is the foundation the later thing depends on (build it properly now, don't half-do it).

### STACK.md requires "why this over alternatives," not just "what was used"
- The user flagged that an early draft of STACK.md's design only covered what/when, not why — and that the why is often the most interview-valuable part.
- **Why:** a stack entry that just names a technology gives an interviewer nothing to ask a follow-up about. The real value is in "chose X over Y because Z" — recognizing a limitation and reacting to it is the actual signal of engineering judgment, not the technology name itself.
- **How to apply:** every non-trivial STACK.md row must either state the alternative/reason inline (if short) or cross-reference the relevant DECISIONS.md entry by date (if long) — never just a bare technology name with a notes field that restates it.

### Status/offer hook added — three states, not a binary
- Considered a simple binary ("show a message every session" vs. "show nothing") for surfacing whether a project has Foundry set up.
- **Why:** a binary doesn't handle the real cases — a project that's already scaffolded shouldn't be re-asked; a project where the user deliberately said no shouldn't be nagged every session either. Three states (scaffolded/dismissed/neither) cover the actual decision space without being presumptuous in someone else's repo or annoying in your own.
- **How to apply:** `foundry.scaffolded`/`foundry.dismissed` are written by `foundry-init`'s own flow (Step 2.5 and the dismiss path respectively) — the status hook only reads them, never writes them, keeping the read/write responsibility cleanly separated between the hook (cheap, runs every session) and the orchestrator (runs once, on a real user action).

### Context-checkpoint discipline added as a standing CLAUDE.md rule
- The user asked whether Foundry enforces good context-management practice (checkpointing, clearing at natural boundaries) the way it enforces verify-before-trust — prompted by noticing this build session itself had run long across several different subtasks without a proactive suggestion to checkpoint.
- **Why:** this is the same category of thing as verify-before-trust — a good practice that depends on an assistant remembering to apply it in the moment is exactly the failure mode the SessionStart hook already exists to prevent for "read the docs." The actual mechanism that makes long-running work reliable is re-anchoring on CLAUDE.md/DECISIONS.md/SESSIONS.md after a clear, not hitting a specific context-percentage number — so the rule is framed around recognizing drift/scope sprawl, not a numeric threshold.
- **How to apply:** added to the CLAUDE.md template's standing Rules section, so every Foundry-scaffolded project carries this expectation by default rather than only the projects where a user happens to ask about it.

### EXPLAIN_MODE added now, not deferred to roadmap
- The user raised that newer developers need to understand *why*, not just *what*, by default — and asked whether this should be built now or deferred since "easy to add" wasn't certain.
- **Why:** Most of the underlying reasoning already existed in each skill's own SKILL.md (each skill already explains its own rationale internally) — the actual gap was surfacing that reasoning to the user in the moment, not authoring new explanations from scratch. That made it cheap to add now: one extra question in `foundry-init`, one short instruction added to each of the 5 sub-skills. A full adaptive/configurable help system (per-skill verbosity levels, persisted preference, etc.) would have been a much bigger scope addition and was correctly identified as not "easy" — that stayed deferred.
- **How to apply:** When a feature request turns out to be "surface reasoning that's already written down" rather than "invent new reasoning," it's usually cheap and worth doing immediately rather than deferring. When it requires new infrastructure (persistence, new config surface, testing both modes thoroughly), treat it as a separate scoped addition.

### Naming: Foundry (tool) under Preamble (brand umbrella)
- Considered renaming the whole project to "Preamble" mid-build after the idea came up in a separate conversation. Decided instead: Foundry stays the name of this specific tool; Preamble becomes the umbrella brand for this and future tools (Promptify, etc.), with a domain like preamblefoundry.com tying them together.
- **Why:** "Foundry" already fits the metaphor (forging the foundational scaffolding a project is built on) precisely, and renaming mid-build for a name that fits a different kind of product (a "preamble" is the framing statement *before* the real content — better suited to a chat/conversation-framing product) would have been rework without a clear improvement.
- **How to apply:** Don't rename Foundry again without a concrete reason; new tools built later can adopt the Preamble umbrella naming pattern (`Preamble<ToolName>` or similar) without touching this repo.

### Configuration derived from a questionnaire, not named presets
- Considered offering named presets (e.g. "base," "advanced," "mobile," "regulated") as a faster path to configuring a new project.
- **Why:** Presets bundle unrelated dimensions (regulatory load, platform, team size) into a single name, which becomes unmaintainable and opaque quickly — nobody remembers what "advanced-mobile" actually configures six months later. A short questionnaire derives the same configuration transparently, traceable to specific yes/no answers.
- **How to apply:** Resist adding named presets later even if requested for convenience — if a fast-path is needed for a common case, prefer a single binary fast-path question (like the existing minimal/throwaway check) over a named preset system.

### Proactive code-review agents deferred to roadmap, not built now
- Considered building dedicated security-review and code-quality-review agents that run proactively (e.g. via `PostToolUse` hooks) as part of this initial build.
- **Why:** This needs its own design pass (blocking vs. advisory, noise tuning, which existing tools to invoke rather than duplicate — e.g. a project's own `/code-review` or security-review skills) and would have expanded this build's scope significantly. Better to ship the core scaffolding solidly and design this properly later than rush both.
- **How to apply:** Listed explicitly in README.md's Roadmap so it isn't lost; don't build it opportunistically inside an unrelated change — give it its own planning pass when picked up.

### Governance sections default to "not yet researched" rather than generic compliance language
- Considered having `foundry-governance` generate generic compliance boilerplate (e.g. a standard "GDPR checklist") to fill a regulatory section when specifics aren't known yet.
- **Why:** Generic-but-plausible compliance text creates false confidence — a reader can't tell it apart from a verified statement, and the gap only becomes visible when it matters (i.e. too late). An explicit, marked placeholder ("not yet researched, get this reviewed by counsel") can't be mistaken for a real guarantee.
- **How to apply:** Standing rule for `foundry-governance`: never fabricate plausible regulatory/compliance content to fill a gap. State the gap plainly instead. Verified in the end-to-end test (ComplianceBot scenario) — the rendered CLAUDE.md explicitly says "NOT YET RESEARCHED" rather than inventing SEC-specific language.

### No community/external skill packs bundled by default
- Considered baking in a specific named third-party skill pack (mentioned informally as something "people recommend") during initial design.
- **Why:** No independent verification was available of what that specific pack actually contains — bundling it on reputation alone would mean recommending something unverified. Foundry is designed to support pluggable external skill packs via Claude Code's plugin/marketplace mechanism, but none are bundled by default.
- **How to apply:** Any future addition of a named external pack should go through independent review of what it actually does first, documented in this log, not added on the strength of a recommendation alone.
